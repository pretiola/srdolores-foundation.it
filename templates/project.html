The Piglet Project in Binzi

Helping families work towards more secure livelihoods through livestock, practical skills and shared responsibility.

Our goal: 60 families

The project aims to support 60 families in Binzi, Uganda with five piglets each. That is a target of 300 piglets, alongside planned start-up feed, care and training.

Small beginnings. Room to grow.

The project uses local Ugandese pigs, a type described in the International Livestock Research Institute’s breed factsheet for Uganda. Usually black, sometimes spotted, these pigs have medium-length bodies, large heads with tapering snouts, medium-to-large ears and long tails.

Hardiness and daily care

ILRI describes local Ugandese pigs as hardy and able to cope with lower-quality feed and challenges such as mange and worms. This resilience does not mean immunity: balanced feeding, clean housing and veterinary care still matter. Shade, fresh water and ventilation help protect them from heat stress.

Steady growth

Local piglets grow slowly. ILRI lists mature females (sows) at 70–120 kg and mature males (boars) at 100–150 kg. These are adult weight ranges, not six-month targets. Families need time, feed and ongoing care to raise their animals; a sale date cannot be promised.

A new generation

Local sows are known for good mothering and fairly high milk production. ILRI describes average-sized litters, with large litters—such as 15 piglets—possible under intensive management, not a typical expectation. Pregnancy in pigs lasts about 114 days; surviving litter size and breeding frequency depend on nutrition, health and care.

With the right care, young pigs can grow into saleable livestock or suitable breeding animals. That potential is why practical training and ongoing care matter as much as providing the first piglets.

Why this work matters

Families need reliable ways to meet everyday needs, including food, school materials, clothing and healthcare. The Piglet Project is intended to help households develop a source of income and strengthen their farming skills.

The families described in the project include widows, single-parent households and households where an older child has taken on a parent's responsibilities. Read about the intended beneficiaries.

How the project is intended to work

  1. Prepare together. Participating families prepare a place to house the piglets and take responsibility for their daily care.
  2. Provide the starting support. The plan includes piglets, start-up feed, feeding and drinking equipment, and veterinary supplies.
  3. Build practical skills. Training and supervision are intended to help families care for the animals. The original plan describes home visits over two months; current arrangements still need confirmation.
  4. Work towards lasting benefits. The intended outcomes are stronger household livelihoods and useful farming experience. Actual results depend on care, costs and local conditions, and need to be documented over time.
